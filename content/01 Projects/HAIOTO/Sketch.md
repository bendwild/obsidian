Nearly 10% of the WHO European region is comprised of international migrants, ranging upwards of 50% in some countries @MigrationHealthResearch

•There is a paucity of research concerning migrant children's perspectives and experiences of health.

- Research illustrating migrant children's perspectives has, to date, focused on a restricted number of health issues.
    
- The overwhelming majority of studies have been concerned with child migrants’ mental health and psychological adaptation.

Research gaps exist regarding other health topics pertinent to migration, such as chronic disease prevention and management, maternal and reproductive health, oral health, nutrition, disaggregated gender and age-related differences, non-refugee migrants, such as migrant workers, and climate-related migration.

Migrant health is a highly dynamic and rapidly developing field. Early research centred on ‘unusual’, often infectious, diseases in minority ethnic groups. Later research trends placed emphasis on the study of biological differences, with a focus on genetically inheritable diseases, before turning to explore behavioural and biological differences at the population level.[6](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/apa.17561#apa17561-bib-0006) All of these approaches carry the risk of inadvertently entrenching the perception of migrants as ‘others’ in contrast to the native population rather than focusing on the unique circumstances of each case

Documented health needs of displaced children include: communicable diseases; incomplete immunisation histories; undiagnosed and poorly managed non-communicable diseases; malnutrition and micronutrient deficiencies; anaemia as a consequence of malnutrition, parasitic infections or [haemoglobinopathy](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/hemoglobinopathy "Learn more about haemoglobinopathy from ScienceDirect's AI-generated Topic Pages"); [musculoskeletal complaints](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/musculoskeletal-disease "Learn more about musculoskeletal complaints from ScienceDirect's AI-generated Topic Pages"); physical trauma; sexually transmitted infections; adolescent pregnancy; [oral disease](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/oral-submucous-fibrosis "Learn more about oral disease from ScienceDirect's AI-generated Topic Pages"); problems associated with [female genital mutilation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/female-genital-mutilation "Learn more about female genital mutilation from ScienceDirect's AI-generated Topic Pages") (practised in some African and Middle Eastern countries); post-traumatic stress disorder; anxiety; depression; internalising and externalising behaviours; and sleep disorders [[7](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666535225000606?via%3Dihub#bib7),[15](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666535225000606?via%3Dihub#bib15)]. Health assessments should also screen for development and disability needs. Health, nutrition, personal environment, and exposure to traumatic events are important determinants of child development therefore adversities associated with forced displacement may interfere with critical stages of a child's social, emotional, physical and intellectual development [[82](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666535225000606?via%3Dihub#bib82),[83](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666535225000606?via%3Dihub#bib83)].

A ‘think-family’ approach should be applied to health assessments of newly arrived accompanied children in recognition that child and parent/caregiver health is inter-related [[88](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666535225000606?via%3Dihub#bib88)].
