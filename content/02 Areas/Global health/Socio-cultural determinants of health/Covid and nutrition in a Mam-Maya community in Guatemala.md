# Reflections

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=3&annotation=667R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.3]]
> > When the coronavirus reached Guatemala, it had a strong impact on food security because of existing difficulties securing basic income

> [!PDF|255, 208, 0] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=4&annotation=670R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.4]]
> > Our research on chronic malnutrition in San Juan questions the tendency to equate food insecurity, chronic malnutrition and stunting, drawing attention to shortcomings in existing global health frameworks for hunger.

> [!PDF|255, 208, 0] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=4&annotation=673R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.4]]
> > As we illustrate, people did not report hunger during the pandemic, although they did have extensive stories of political and economic hardship

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=5&annotation=676R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.5]]
> > We argue that as scientists work to evaluate hunger and malnutrition they must incorporate Indigenous knowledge into the science of global health

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=7&annotation=679R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.7]]
> > We thought that the quotidian, widespread food insecurity documented by development organizations would have been amplified during the pandemic, offering a meaningful opportunity to discuss the experience of living with chronic malnutrition. However, upon beginning our interviews, we quickly learned that we would have to adjust our expectations. The people we spoke with did not report feeling hunger

> [!PDF|187, 97, 229] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=7&annotation=682R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.7]]
> > The background question that animated our research was  What is chronic malnutrition for the people living through it?

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=7&annotation=685R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.7]]
> > The  chronicity  of chronic malnutrition normalizes it, making it imperceptible. This, in turn, offers a challenge for anthropo- logical methods since they tend to rely on lived experience.

> [!PDF|255, 208, 0] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=10&annotation=688R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.10]]
> > He explained that ‘alimento’ imposed a colonialist, nutrient-based ethos over food, encouraging people to think of eating in terms of vitamin and mineral content – not in terms of the impacts on community relations, for example

> [!PDF|255, 208, 0] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=10&annotation=691R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.10]]
> > Meanwhile, he found that when Indigenous people categorized a food as  alimento  they were noting that its purchase required their labor to be ‘siphoned off by the regional market system into Ladino [non-Indigenous] channels

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=13&annotation=694R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.13]]
> > When we asked if they stayed at home when the country went into lockdown, they said this would be impossible. They were afraid of sickness, but foregoing a cycle of planting would mean certain death. Similarly, women tended not to isolate themselves from their aging mothers

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=16&annotation=697R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.16]]
> > By all accounts, it is money sent from abroad from family members – not government support – that keeps the communities of San Juan going.

> [!PDF|187, 97, 229] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=16&annotation=700R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.16]]
> > The pandemic had not left them hungry. Their maize was strong; they knew how to feed themselves. Instead, they were leaving their homelands because the pandemic had exacerbated already difficult social conditions including economic discrimination and violence against Maya people. People generally did not want to leave, but they saw no other choice.

> [!PDF|187, 97, 229] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=16&annotation=703R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.16]]
> > Chronic malnutrition is an indicator that measures a situation, not of children, but of societies.

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=17&annotation=706R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.17]]
> > Western scientists have long held that Indigenous Guatemalan diets are deficient in nutrients.

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=17&annotation=709R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.17]]
> > Some degree of micronutrient deficiency may have existed in the ancestral Guatemalan diet (iodizing salt has helped to prevent goiter; as has adding vitamin A to sugar helped to reduce cases of blindness; see Ruz and Solomons  2019; Arroyave et al.  1981). But it is also the case that the corn-bean-squash-chile based diet, the staple food of Indigenous communities, is remarkably healthy in terms of nutrient composition, containing the nutrients people need not just to live but to thrive

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=18&annotation=712R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.18]]
> > Rather, he suggested that what is imprecisely called chronic malnutrition is not caused by a deficiency in available nutrients but rather by an environmental trigger that he referred to as ‘stress’ (see also Solomons 2019). He explained, ‘the problem that women and children experience is that of early life “stress exposures” that can compromize physiological development’. The problem that policymakers are calling  chronic malnutri- tion  does not actually relate to nutrient intake, and the problem ‘will only go away when these environmental stressors are overcome’

> [!PDF|187, 97, 229] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=18&annotation=715R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.18]]
> > In other words, shortness on its own is not a problem and it should not be equated to chronic malnutrition

> [!PDF|187, 97, 229] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=18&annotation=718R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.18]]
> > Her reframing of  xwoky as delicious – not deficient – helps us to see that the problem scientists were calling chronic malnutrition was not a problem of Maya physiology or Maya foods but a problem of colonial dispossession and the brutality of labor that Indigenous people were forced, by law, to undertake. The science of chronic malnutrition has tended to focus on inadequacy of bodies and diets. In naming  xwoky  as a food of abundance, Mercedes was helping us to recenter our stories of malnutrition on the harms of a political and economic system that saw Maya bodies as expendable and did not care if Maya people died

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=19&annotation=721R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.19]]
> > When describing the hardships they encountered in the pandemic, people we interviewed in San Juan kept their focus squarely on conditions of labor and land. They spoke of the pandemic as a time of challenge and difficulty, but the  chronicity  of chronic malnutrition lay in the persistence of political structures that disenfranchized Maya people – not in the persistence of bodily hunger

> [!PDF|187, 97, 229] [[YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025.pdf#page=20&annotation=724R|YatesDoerr-Hungerschronicities-2025, p.20]]
> > People in San Juan know how to care for hunger – they do so through their land and community. But today, much of their community has traveled so far away that they no longer have access to xwoky  to extinguish the fire in their bellies. Without community there can be no maize and without maize there can be no community. Without both they will find themselves in a time of absence – a season of hunger, la falta de, uti txilil wuaj.
