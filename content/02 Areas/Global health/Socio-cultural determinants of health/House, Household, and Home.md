> > Ultimately, we suggest that experiences of postearthquake reconstruction have highlighted conceptual difﬁculties with existing legal deﬁnitions of ownership and residence and chal- lenged people’s sense of belonging at the affective level. This is an ironic outcome for a program that was promoted as an “owner-driven”  model of reconstruction,  3  pushing us to think further about the deﬁnition of  “ownership”  and the material and social units in relation to which it is deﬁned. We argue that careful attention to deﬁnitions of  “house,” “household,”  and “home”  in both anthropological and policy contexts matters because those terminologies drive material and social pro- cesses of change that may begin in moments of natural disaster but extend far beyond that temporal horizon to reconﬁgure the very shape of communities themselves

> > While speciﬁc requirements for reconstruction may have differed by location, houses acted as infrastructural mediators between state policies and citizen practices across our  ﬁeld sites. This assertion builds on insights from feminist geogra- phy, which clariﬁes that the  “social reproduction of the ma- terial bases of society involves not only the large-scale physical infrastructure of capitalism . . . but the small-scale social, physical, cultural and emotional infrastructure of the house- hold”  (Marston 2000:233). Even before the 2015 earthquakes, houses in all of our sites could be understood as both infra- structure and process, produced through the continual labor of maintenance (Besky 2017), rather than as  ﬁxed and un- changing entities
