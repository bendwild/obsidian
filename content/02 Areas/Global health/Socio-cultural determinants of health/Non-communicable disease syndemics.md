# Reflections

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes.pdf#page=1&annotation=973R|Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes, p.951]]
> > The co-occurrence of health burdens in transitioning populations, particularly in specific socioeconomic and cultural contexts, calls for conceptual frameworks to improve understanding of risk factors, so as to better design and implement prevention and intervention programmes to address comorbidities. The concept of a syndemic,  developed by medical anthropologists, provides such a framework for preventing and treating  comorbidities.

> [!PDF|187, 97, 229] [[Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes.pdf#page=1&annotation=976R|Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes, p.951]]
> > Key messages 
> > •  Non-communicable  diseases  share  common  risk  factors resulting in escalation of comorbidities, especially among low-income,  marginalised  populations  worldwide 
> > •  The  clustering  of  social  and  health  problems  is  often overlooked in social [[epidemiology]] and other models of epidemiological transition 
> > •  Syndemic  care  requires  that  we  recognise  how  social problems cluster with and affect medical problems, and that co-occurring  diseases  can  present  differently  than  singular disorders

## Diabetes syndemics in rapidly transitioning economies
> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes.pdf#page=4&annotation=985R|Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes, p.954]]
> > Broadly, epidemiologists have shown that rapid economic growth has contributed to demographic, nutrition, and health transitions that have extensively  shaped  the  incidence  and  prevalence  of obesity.9,61  Such  transitions  come  together  through technological innovations, labour opportunities, rural-to- urban migration, access to education, and social mobility, which greatly transform how people think, move, and nourish their bodies.
> 
> 

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes.pdf#page=4&annotation=988R|Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes, p.954]]
> > Epidemiological  transition  of  NCDs  to  low-income populations in LMICs poses a complex medical context in which NCDs interact with infectious chronic diseases, such as [[HIV]]/AIDS and [[tuberculosis]]

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes.pdf#page=4&annotation=991R|Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes, p.954]]
> > Local  contexts  influence  syndemic  interaction  of diabetes with diseases of poverty and must be recognised in the design and implementation of interventions. For example, structural factors influence the emergence of NCDs by affecting not only what people eat,39  but also how people move securely in the world

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes.pdf#page=5&annotation=994R|Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes, p.955]]
> > Globally,  diabetes  intersects  with  chronic  depression more frequently in low-income populations because of the strong relationship between depression and poverty111 and the stresses linked to poor access to, and extraordinary costs of, diabetes care

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes.pdf#page=5&annotation=997R|Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes, p.955]]
> > Therefore, there is substantial evidence that the interactions and outcomes of depression and diabetes are mediated by social contexts,8,115  and therefore are  particularly  devastating  among  low-income populations in HICs116  and LMICs
> 
> 

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes.pdf#page=6&annotation=1000R|Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes, p.956]]
> > Financing of health systems has a fundamental role in modelling  syndemics  because  it  determines  which diseases are prioritised, tested, and treated in primary care settings.

## Application of syndemic models to improve global health
> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes.pdf#page=8&annotation=1003R|Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes, p.958]]
> > Syndemics  render prevention  and  intervention  programmes  more successful when addressing the multiple disorders and specific contextual vulnerabilities holistically, rather than viewing the disorders individually or as extractable from the context in which they occur

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes.pdf#page=8&annotation=1006R|Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes, p.958]]
> > Many physicians simply believe that structural and social interventions are “not our job”,141  a  belief  that  points  to  how  clinical  training explicitly focuses on diagnosis and treatment without considering  how  social  problems  affect  medical problems.142  Moreover,  with  increasing  complexity  of patient care, there is increased risk of negative sequelae for both patients and health-care providers.143

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes.pdf#page=9&annotation=1009R|Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes, p.959]]
> > Task-sharing  provides  an  exemplar  system through which syndemic care is delivered; however, it requires training for holistic health models as opposed to singular diseases (as is traditionally delivered). Therefore, instead of one community health worker for [[malaria]], and another for mental health, community health workers would receive syndemic care training.
> 
> 

## Recommendations and steps forward
> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes.pdf#page=10&annotation=1012R|Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes, p.960]]
> > First, researchers and care providers must recognise how social and medical problems cluster and interact within certain populations.

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes.pdf#page=10&annotation=1015R|Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes, p.960]]
> > Second, funding institutions must provide opportunities for research and data that use a syndemic framework

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes.pdf#page=10&annotation=1018R|Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes, p.960]]
> > Third, more evidence for the effectiveness of integrative care and task-sharing across health workers is needed to detect syndemic problems and deliver syndemic care

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes.pdf#page=10&annotation=1021R|Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes, p.960]]
> > Fourth, randomised controlled trials will need to evaluate the syndemic model as a tool for improving outcomes and  to  determine  if  the  syndemic  (re)orientation  is effective.

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes.pdf#page=10&annotation=1024R|Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes, p.960]]
> > Fifth, on the basis of the aforementioned steps, policy change will be necessary to move beyond singular disease  models  and  incorporate  more  integrative syndemic frameworks of disease emergence, interaction, and  subsequent  adverse  outcomes.

> [!PDF|234, 82, 82] [[Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes.pdf#page=10&annotation=1027R|Non-communicable disease syndemics poverty, depression, and diabetes, p.960]]
> > Finally, implementation of syndemic care will provide people- centred medical care that does not align health-care delivery  with  international  donor  politics  (thereby prioritising one disease, such as [[HIV]], [[tuberculosis]], or [[malaria]], more than diseases marginalised by aid funding
