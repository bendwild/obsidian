= research method used to **comprehensively identify and rigorously synthesize** relevant evidence to answer a specific research question 
- structured (has logical sequence)
- reproducible (another researcher could repeat it)
- transparent (all steps are fully documented)
- pre-defined (no post-hoc judgements)

# Relevance
How to find the needle in the haystack?
- increasing numbers of publications
- diversity in publications (languages, settings)
- contradictory results of studies
- heterogeneity in study types and data validity

Use of systematic reviews
- establish clinical effectiveness
- inform health and public policies
- inform clinical guidelines
- identify gaps in knowledge and set future research agenda
- justify grant funding for research 
- provide structured way to disseminate knowledge
- inform disease modelling
- …

> [!important] When do systematic reviews add most value?
> 1. There is an **impactful research or policy question** AND
> 2. There are several **primary studies** BUT
> 3. There are **uncertainties** about the findings

# How?
Main steps for conducting a systematic review
1. Formulate research question(s) (PICO framework)
2. Plan the review (via a protocol = work-plan for the systematic review)
3. Comprehensive search
	1. develop search strategy
	2. minimum 2 databases
4. Selection of studies
5. Data extraction
6. Standardization of data
7. Assess risk of [[bias]]
	1. Ascertain the **validity of results of primary studies** (how much can you trust the findings)
	2. By identifying flaws in design and execution of these studies
	3. CAVE depends on quality of reporting
8. Data synthesis (may include meta-analysis)
	1. Provide summary findings based on aggregate data, depending on
		1. objective of systematic review
		2. type of included studies and data
		3. quality of available data
		4. quantity of available data
	2. Can be done through meta-analysis, however not always necessary
9. Interpretation of results
10. Reporting

> [!ATTENTION] Meta-analysis
> = statistical methods to combine and weight quantitative data from primary studies that are: 
> - sufficiently comparable
> - have low risk of [[bias]]

# Other methods

# Limitations
- depends on quality and quantity of existing data
- depends on quality of **reporting**
- prone to errors
- time consuming and labor-intensive
- reports may still be inconclusive
- parts of analysis are subjective (e.g., risk of [[bias]] assessment)
- never 100% sure that all studies are captured
- requires periodic updating
- **reporting [[bias]]**; studies with significant, positive findings are more likely to be
	- published
	- published in English
	- published rapidly
	- published in a non-obscure journal
	- cited
	- published more than once
