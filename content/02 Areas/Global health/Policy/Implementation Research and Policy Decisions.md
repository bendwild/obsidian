# Reflections

Implementation research is long on description and short on prescription. 

Most implementation research is case studies. A fact, by itself, neither good nor bad. But it creates advice that is often desultory and strategically vague. 

Two distinguishable approaches to implementation analysis

## Backward Mapping and Implementation Research
Backward mapping stresses the dispersal of control and concentrates on factors that can only be **indirectly influenced by policymakers**
- knowledge and problem-solving ability of lower-level administrators
- incentive structures that operate on the subjects of policy
- bargaining relationships among political actors at various levels of the implementation process
- strategic use of funds to affect discretionary choice

Except in cases where a policy requires strict performance of a highly structured routine (airline safety for example), strong hierarchical controls work against the principle of reciprocity, that formal authority in the form of policy statements is heavily dependent upon specialized problem-solving capabilities further down the chain of authority and vice-versa

Inverse relationship between the number of transactions required to implement a decision and the likelihood that an effect, any effect, would result. 

The tighter the structure of hierarchical relationships, the greater the number of checks and decision points required to assure compliance. The harder we try, using conventional tools of hierarchical control, the less likely we are to achieve. 

Forward mapping **reinforces the pathologies of hierarchy**

When implementation consists essentially of controlling discretion, the effect is to reduce reliance on knowledge and skill at the delivery level and increase reliance on abstract, standardized solutions. 

Impact depends upon the existence of “effective bargaining arenas”, in which the competing demands of local groups could be worked out. 

Implementation literature provides strong support for an analytic framework that takes account of: 
- reciprocity in the relationship between superiors and subordinates
- the connection between hierarchical control and increased complexity
- discretion as an adaptive device
- bargaining as a precondition for local effects

## Backward Mapping on Youth Employment Policy
At least three sets of delivery-level decisions are important in determining the effects of youth employment policy: 
1. service providers’ decisions about who gets access to which services
2. young peoples’ decisions to enter particular kinds of training and to seek particular jobs
3. employers’ decisions to fill entry-level positions

These decisions cannot be standardized, managed, or controlled using conventional administrative tools. 

The logic of backward mapping connects policy decisions directly with the point at which their effect occurs.
