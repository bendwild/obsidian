# Reflections

> Neither health nor healthcare ‘just happens’

## Simple truths
1. Healthcare systems want to grow
	1. at the root of this expansionist dynamic lies a potent interaction among the desires and expectations of recipients and producers of services
	2. with so many expansionist forces afoot, governments face a very challenging environment in which to manage healthcare resources effectively
2. Higher health spending does not necessarily lead to higher health status
3. Universal access to health care does not lead to universally good health
4. Public awareness of risk to health has greatly improved
	1. perhaps the greatest contribution to health promotion over the past two decades has been to raise public awareness about individual risk factors
5. Healthcare almost always wins out in the competition for resources (in comparison to health promotion or disease prevention f.e.)
	1. pouring resources into health care generates short-term political capital because it responds to highly visible and viscerally felt needs, and expands the domain and security of providers
6. Changing the distribution of health status through upstream strategies is extraordinarily difficult
	1. evidence suggest that individually targeted health promotion and disease prevention tends to be more effective for higher SES
	2. data show that it is far easier to improve population health status differentially than to equalize it

## A few less simple challenges
1. Do healthy individuals invariably create health communities, or vice-versa?
2. Identifying disparities in health status does not mean there is consensus to eliminate them
3. The temporal challenge
	1. people and their governments value current over future benefits
4. The epistemological dilemma
	1. the evidence for health-oriented social policy is epistemologically less solid than the evidence arising from controlled clinical trials ([[When metrics take over]])
	2. the evidence in the social policy sphere is almost always correlational
5. Knowledge and gridlock
	1. a simple and straightforward understanding of health can be empowering; conversely, knowledge of the complex determinants of health can lead to policy paralysis
	2. if wealth, status, power, and their distribution largely determine the distribution of health, can any health policies, in the end, create effects independent of general economic and political policies?
6. The redistribution dilemma
7. The power of belief systems
	1. people’s beliefs about what is important and what is not, and what works and what does not, may influence health policy far more profoundly than research-based reasoning
	2. although these beliefs are fundamental, they need not be rigid, human action and leadership can change them

## The health information agenda
Health information systems have been developed principally to administer healthcare systems. As such they are in the main misnamed; they have not been about health, but about sickness and the units of service deployed to address it. A genuine health record would look much different if the goal were to assemble the data elements essential to understanding individual’s health status over the course of a life. 

The compelling tales to be told about the accumulating evidence on [[Health and Wealth]] and social characteristics are all too often confined to the seminar room and the learned journals

There are three main audiences for the population health policy message
- politicians
- the general public
- the workplace and corporations 

## Taking accountability seriously
There is considerable, perhaps excessive, accountability for health care, but not for health!

Governments may be disinclined to commit themselves to health goals for several reasons: 
- there may be no consensus on what those goals should be
- they may perceive their own powerlessness to change health
- they may be forces to spend money on health care that they know will not improve health status even while it generates political capital

The difficulty lies in the secular trends: health status seems to be getting better and no one knows exactly why. If the trend continues, governments may be tempted to claim credit for improvements despite an inability to connect the outcomes with anything they did. 

There is neither general nor system-specific accountability for health status

It remains important to intensify scrutiny on how healthcare dollars are spent and subject healthcare to a stricter accountability. **The more healthcare is considered both an essential service, and a commodity to be consumed in ever-greater quantities by wealth societies, the harder it is to constrain.**

## Communicating the civic dimensions of health
How to succeed?
1. The abandonment of comforting assumptions
	1. don’t assume societal consensus that we should invariably act on the determinants of health to improve population health status
2. Sound strategic thinking about health policy in the context of democratic politics
3. Focus on smaller, defined, specific initiatives that have made a difference
	1. avoid the impact fallacy!

> Reconfiguring health policy is substantially about extending and enriching democratic participation
