# Brief summary

In this short but powerful memoir, Pacifique Irankunda recounts his experiences growing up during the 13-year civil war in Burundi. Through the eyes of his childhood self, he narrates the violence, fear, and trauma caused by the ethnic conflict between the Hutus and Tutsis. Every chapter is a different part of his survival story.  How he and his brother spent nights hiding in the forest for survival, how his village was destroyed by the war, how his classmates were killed by militia men, and ultimately, how his home country, the country of milk and honey, will never be the same again. Despite this, he conveys the message that healing is possible, in his case, through writing, and that hope is necessary for Burundi and Burundians to move beyond just survival.

# Extensive summary

The book commences with Irankunda’s recollections of a time before the war, the only blissful part of his childhood. He introduces us to his family and their dynamics, with his mother (Maman Clémence) as the central character. She is introduced as an unending source of hope and kindness in a context where these values were hard to find.

At 4 years old, the initial peace he describes was shattered after the assassination of Burundi’s Hutu president. The event triggered widespread violence between the ethnic groups, eventually leading to a civil war that would last 13 years. As he and his family belonged to the Tutsi minority, they quickly became targets of hostility and violence. Soon, they are forced to flee their home for survival. Despite these conditions, Maman Clémence insists that her children don’t lose hope and continue their education.

He describes the emotional and psychological effects of growing up during war. He, and many children alike, personally witnessed violence, lost friends and relatives, and were forced to grow up quickly in order to survive. In one particularly tragic episode, a school that attempts to promote unity between ethnic groups is attacked by militia forces. Ordered at gunpoint, students were forced to separate by ethnicity. However, students refused to comply with the orders and chose to affirm their common identity as children of God, resulting in the massacre of these students. Irankunda makes it out alive, with many of his classmates dying at the hands of the militia.  

Irankunda also reflects on Burundi’s traditional institutions, particularly the Bashigantahe. He describes these as institutions of wisdom, a way of organizing societies that is absent in modern nation-states. In Burundi’s old kingdom, villages recognized individuals known for their wisdom and justice, and gave them power as mediators of the king and enforcers of value. German and Belgian colonial administrations destroyed these old institutions in favor of Western power structures. The Belgians, in particular, manipulated ethnic identities within these power structures, divisions that eventually contributed to the ethnic violence in which the author grew up.

Eventually, partly thanks to the teachings of Maman Clémence, Irankunda receives an opportunity to continue his education in the United States. Here, he is exposed to a completely different environment. One striking example is when one of his schoolmates at Deerfield Academy is playing a wargame, something that deeply troubles him, as someone who was exposed to the realities of war. Throughout this time, he struggles to adapt to his new life while still carrying the memories of his traumatic childhood.

He also discusses his search for identity and belonging in the aftermath of the war and his move to the US. Despite the trauma, he continues to feel deeply connected to Burundi and his childhood. Cultural symbols that represent his heritage, such as the traditional Watusi cattle (the cattle of kings), give him some solace in the thought that these cattle now exist across different continents.

Writing became an important part of Irankunda’s healing process. By telling his story, he can better confront painful memories and transform them into something meaningful. This is also his way to preserve the art of storytelling that was once shared among families and communities in Burundi. Through writing, he hopes to honor the wisdom of his elders and pass their lessons on to future generations. For Burundi and Burundians, Irankunda wants more than survival, and this is his part in the story.

# Critical reflection

This book is hard to criticize, as it is a humble and brave retelling of his life and reflections. I will do my best to highlight some points that struck me.

While Irakunda is a great writer, the nature of this piece makes his book's narrative structure extremely fractured. He guides us through experiences of the war, his experiences in the US, and everything in between, but makes it (I would argue intentionally) very hard for the reader to follow. This made writing the extensive summary unexpectedly difficult, as I had to substantially shift the chronology. The book reads like it wasn't written for an outsider to the war, maybe not even for an insider. It feels more like proof on paper of the struggle with his past, written to detangle his own trauma.

This, in itself, is not a bad thing; it is very much in the nature of a memoir. We get a personal look at his own struggles, beyond the war. I found his perspective extremely interesting and humbling; however, it is, in many ways, not an accurate representation of the civil war in Burundi. First of all, Burundi, as he tells us, was extremely rose-colored before the war. He refers to the country multiple times as the land of milk and honey, with little attention to the struggles the country, or his family, faced before. Additionally, he sidesteps the roots of the conflicts by discussing German and Belgian colonization only briefly. In describing the horrors of the genocide, they almost feel like a sidenote, not in their gravity, but more in the limelight they are given in the book. Additionally, not once is there a mention of, for example, (sexual) violence against women. Thus, his interesting perspective has important caveats, which readers experienced with the country, conflict, or the genocide in neighbouring Rwanda will be aware of. However, new readers might leave with a limited perspective on this important history.

Furthermore, while he provides a very interesting analysis of Burundi's former institutional structures (Bashigantahe), he does not go very in-depth on this topic. He mentions multiple times in this book that he wonders how Burundi would have been if the Bashigante were still there. If the colonizers had not destroyed this heritage, would the civil war have happened? I wish he had gone more in-depth on what these institutions of wisdom could look like in modern-day Burundi, or how the tradition of storytelling could be restored.
