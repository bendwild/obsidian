Over the past decades, the dominant paradigm shaping healthcare improvements in hospitals and healthcare organizations has been quality improvement (QI).1,2 Governments, administrators, and managers increasingly demand evidence of safe practices, efficient resource use, and improved patient care. QI provides a practical, reproducible approach to assess safety or value gaps in care and enhance service delivery and outcomes.3,4 It is a coordinated, iterative, and data-driven approach grounded in quantitative methods, aimed at providing high-quality patient care and improving population health.5 QI translates this broad goal into defined, measurable, and actionable aims that, together, have the potential to improve quality of care at the system level.1,3

QI emerged from historical traditions of statistical governance and social measurement. One of the earliest examples of this can be found in the book “_Natural and Political Observations… Made Upon the Bills of Mortality_” (1662). Using the bills of mortality, John Graunt predicted life expectancy, community economic losses, and mortality rates in terms of survivorship.6 200 years later, Florence Nightingale demonstrated through quantitative experiments that sanitation and hygiene standards reduced mortality among soldiers wounded in the Crimean War.7 Another half-century later, Ernest Amory Codman introduced “End Result Cards” to determine whether the treatments provided were successful. These cards were a novel way to track patient demographics, diagnoses, treatments, and outcomes, and to prevent new failures.8 These were the precursors to modern-day evidence-based medicine and eventually to QI, as they formally integrated [[data collection]] and trend analysis as comprehensive measures for quality monitoring. The field has undergone various iterations since, as QI draws on a wide variety of approaches and methods, with the most commonly used analytical methods today stemming from the work of W. Edwards Deming.5,9

The necessity for quality improvement in (global) health became clear as recognition grew that access to healthcare is not enough. In 2018, _The Lancet Global Health Commission on High Quality Health Systems_ found that 60% of all lives lost from treatable conditions were people who actually obtained access to care.10 Hospital-acquired infections, misdiagnosed myocardial infarctions, and antimicrobial resistance are some of the countless examples of how health systems fail to provide quality care.

The concept of QI thus appears to be an intuitive solution to improve outcomes. The sheer number of coverage and outcome indicators in the Millennium Development Goals and now the Sustainable Development Goals reflects this ambition for data-driven QI. Countless interventions are in place to improve these indicators; however, with varying success. QI interventions are too often seen as “_magic bullets_” that will improve outcomes with little regard to context, despite little progress in healthcare quality over the past decades.11,12 As Dr Kaveh Shojania put it, “_We have more names for the field and more frameworks than we have effective interventions_”.13

Additionally, the narrow focus, short-term, and limited scale, inherent to the need for QI to be measurable and specific, contribute to the minimal population impact. Various actors and health ministries complain that the many, often overlapping, QI programs in place are difficult to manage and evaluate, and are impossible to scale, as they have become largely technical activities that depend on the operationalization of quality concepts.14,15

These challenges invite us to critically assess QI. While the ideology of QI, giving the people closest to issues affecting care quality the agency and resources they need to solve them, is hard to argue, the obsessive need to measure and standardize creates many of the issues we see today.5 Similar to the argument Vincanne Adams makes in her chapter on evidence-based global public health, the prioritization of performance over participants’ lived experiences and the relegation of what cannot be measured to obscurity inherently limits the value of these systems. Once a certain method becomes the dominant way of being, it gains acceptance not only as the desirable but also as the only legitimate approach, and institutionalizes a unidimensional approach to healthcare.

Additionally, we need to ask ourselves the question “How do we decide what to improve in QI?”. The historical neglect of prevention and socio-economic determinants of health seeps into how we develop QI interventions. Most QI interventions focus on clinical care, optimizing medical workflows, and improving technical efficiency, rather than addressing upstream drivers of disease. This pattern is reproduced in the biomedical industrial complex, as concrete progress focuses on the development of highly specialized imaging equipment, tertiary prevention treatments, and expensive personalized medicine. A renewed focus and agency for the _[[Pareto principle]]_, the 20% that causes 80% of the burden, could substantially improve the effectiveness of QI over the coming years.

To make it clear, QI is not a dead end. It plays a key role in strengthening health systems worldwide, but it is far from sufficient. Other methods are needed to complement QI, along with a _“cultural reset_” to redefine [[global health priorities]]. Additionally, some critiques of QI can also be seen as strengths. The difficulty of scaling, as mentioned above, points back to [[the tyranny of the global]] in Yates-Doerr et al.16 Instead of seeing this as a limitation, we can accept that not everything needs to be standardized for globalization and ensure QI projects give greater consideration to [[local biologies]].

A practical application of this analysis on QI can be found in my capstone project. In summary, the project focuses on exploring the intersection of [[sepsis]] and malnutrition to support (early) risk stratification and the development of better clinical guidelines in Tanzania. As pediatric [[sepsis]] management is highly contextual, the development and implementation of context-appropriate, evidence-based [[sepsis]] programs could be particularly relevant for the country.17

However, a critic might interject on the true value of this intervention. While noble in its goals, at the end of the day, it focuses on a very limited subset of the population (children presenting to the hospital with malnutrition and [[sepsis]]). To give some context, there is only one public facility in Tanzania that offers pediatric subspecialty care by pediatricians with advanced training in emergency medicine, and that hospital receives around 100-200 pediatric [[sepsis]] cases each month.18,19 Of these children, the intervention would target only those with malnutrition. While clinically relevant, the project will have a very limited impact on absolute hospital-level mortality/morbidity, if successful. In the meantime, significant resources are being invested in this project, as it requires pathogen identification, gene sequencing, transcriptomic analysis, and more.

To quote Sandro Galeo, “_I think one of our challenges is that we keep mixing up health and health care_”.  Would it not be better to use the resources to prevent malnutrition rather than manage the downstream effects of socio-economic deprivation on health? From the perspective of the patient, does it not matter more to be well-fed than to receive marginally better care in the one specialized hospital in the country?  As long as QI interventions confine themselves to the realm of healthcare, valuing the treatment of the health consequences of socio-economic deprivation over addressing underlying and intermediary drivers, their impact will be limited by the perpetuation of the very conditions that reproduce harm.

Mitchell et al propose a more explicit and expansive value pluralism in healthcare decision-making.15 This includes recognizing that not all benefits are readily measurable or will satisfy managerial and institutional concerns about the cost-efficiency of clinical effectiveness. We need to reclaim QI as a tool that serves patients' health, not the needs of healthcare facilities. QI, as well as clinicians and researchers, need to break free of the shackles of narrow endpoints.

However, we cannot be naïve and ignore the institutional legitimacy and potential funding for QI. But perhaps we can rethink our focus. At the end of the day, QI projects are only as strong as the values that guide what we choose to measure, what we choose to prioritize, and whose lives we ultimately influence.
