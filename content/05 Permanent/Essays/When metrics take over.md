Vincanne Adams and colleagues challenge the dominant paradigm of research and [[knowledge production]] in their 2013 book, “_Critical Global Health: Evidence, Efficacy, Ethnography_.”1 While recognizing the value of rigorous statistical methods, the authors note several concerns about how evidence-based medicine (EBM) and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have been institutionalized in global health research. First and foremost, the chapter argues that often what is lost in the cogs of the statistical machine are the people. They ask whether global health research still serves people in need or has evolved into an industry catering to the demands of generalizability, cost-effectiveness, and audit culture. The authors bring up a particularly poignant example of how a research project on safe motherhood in rural Tibet was defunded because it was not an ideal target for an RCT. Another interesting note that struck home for me is that elements that cannot be measured within RCTs are relegated to the background as merely “_static_”, despite global health being inextricably tied to the social, political, and cultural spheres. As Hacking notes, many phenomena in the world of global public health are always “_on the move_”.

In “_[[Slow Research]]_”, Adams et al introduce an alternative to the current way of doing research in Global Health.2 As its name suggests, [[slow research]] denounces the assumption that speed is success.  Central to this approach is a push away from the constant need to take an intervention that works in one place and scale it up in other global settings. A renewed focus on “_[[Local biologies]]_” provides a framework to rethink this “_Tyranny of the global”._ Recognizing that positive health outcomes don’t always align with global health research needs, [[slow research]] calls for caution. It allows exploratory or generative methods, and the unanticipated, to drive and reconfigure research itself.

As a medical doctor, I have been praying at the altar of EBM for the better part of the last decade. We learn to trust in power, alpha, and confidence intervals, and the body of evidence derived from this process has enabled us to diagnose and treat with a certain degree of certainty, something very needed in a discipline that remains more subjective than many think. However, I have also seen how this hunger for certainty clouds clinical practice. When a patient presents with depressive symptoms, diagnostic checklists quickly structure our thinking, transforming context-rich lived experiences into a series of countable criteria. This insinuates that the patient's story matters less than the metrics we are trying to gather. As medicine is human and messy, few dare to deviate from the scriptures of EBM. However, just as Adams posed before, it begs the question: “Does this knowledge system assist the medical industry or the patients?” I am happy to read something that eloquently puts these observations into words.

Global health, even more than clinical medicine, suffers from these issues. While [[slow research]] offers a new approach, I don’t believe the solution lies in throwing out the old and bringing in a new system to reign. EBM and RCTs are not inherently bad. It is the way these methods have taken over not only health but also our way of thinking that is alarming. Receiving higher education in the rationalist, neo-liberal system of most Western countries means you are taught from a young age that everything can be solved with science, and that science is about statistical evidence and experiments. This has created a generation, myself included, of well-intentioned professionals who lack the epistemic humility required to admit the limits of statistical evidence and recognize when different forms of knowledge are needed.3 A renewed educational and intellectual framework is needed.
