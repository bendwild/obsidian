_Dyin’ fair._

A Bajan(#_ftn1) phrase underpinning the social contract of Barbados. It refers to the idea that everything, first and foremost, must be embedded in equity. These values of social justice are rooted in a history of colonial conquest and racial violence. Barbados was Britain’s colonial crown jewel, a sugar-producing economic powerhouse built upon the exploitation of its land and people.(#_ftn2) In 1661, Barbados passed one of the first comprehensive slave codes, which became a blueprint for racial slavery across the Americas.(#_ftn3) It was only in 2021 that Barbados fully shed its colonial shackles with the abdication of Queen Elizabeth II as head of state.(#_ftn4) Yet political independence did not dissolve the structures colonizing powers had left behind.

As the largest institutional consumer of hydrocarbons(#_ftn5) started the year with military operations in the largest(#_ftn6), and fourth-largest(#_ftn7) proven oil reserves in the world, we can not deny that the world still revolves around the black tar. Like an addict promising to quit tomorrow,  the petro-capitalist continues to assault the intimate relationship between human life and the environment. A toxic relationship that birthed a child called climate change.

Today, one of the most dangerous legacies of empire, and the single biggest threat facing the country in the 21st century, is climate change.

While we might be weathering the same storm, we are not all in the same boat. For small island developing states (SIDS) such as Barbados, climate change is not an abstract or faraway threat.(#_ftn8) Rising seas, stronger storms, coastal erosion, and floods threaten the lives and livelihoods of millions of Barbadians(#_ftn9), and beyond that, the literal existence of the nation.(#_ftn10)  Meanwhile, Barbados is responsible for only 0.01% of historical global greenhouse gas emissions.(#_ftn11)

This profound asymmetry is not accidental. and can be understood through the framework of racial capitalism. The term refers to the profound interconnectedness of racial domination and economic exploitation, as the end-goal of profit-making institutionalizes racial hierarchies.(#_ftn12) From plantation agriculture to fossil fuel extraction, racialized populations and their land have been positioned as expendable labor forces and green sacrifice zones.(#_ftn13)

The climate crisis and its effects are thus not simply a by-product of modernity but rather the predictable outcome of eco-imperial logics, in which land, people, and resources are treated as assets to serve the indefinite accumulation of capital in a finite world. The same global economy that once enriched empire through Caribbean sugar now enriches corporations through fossil fuels, while frontline nations shoulder the consequences.

Perhaps there are necessary, and even profitable, sacrifices for the continuation of the status quo. And perhaps, these sacrifices consist mostly of Black and Brown bodies.

Critics will point to climate finance and aid, or how this so-called racial capitalism still benefits these nations. However, these arguments are inherently flawed. First of all, the scale of current funding is nowhere near proportional to the harm done, current or historical. Additionally, just as the [[imperial core]] continues to benefit from its hegemony in a decolonial world(#_ftn14), countries affected by climate change have become prime targets for investors and asset managers, as well as respected international monetary organizations such as the IMF, trapping nations in debt cycles and structural adjustment reforms.(#_ftn15)

How could a future governed by many of the same forces as the latter prove to be different? What does environmental justice mean in the face of these realities?

Environmental justice centers fairness in the distribution of environmental benefits and burdens, and the processes that shape those distributions.(#_ftn16) While the Paris Climate Agreement was the first climate treaty to integrate this justice lens, it fails to provide real substance in its policy negotiations.#_ftn17) Specifically, adopting 1.5°C of warming as a target metric does not adequately capture other climate risks or regional variations in sea-level rise, and treats all nations as if they have the same risk or adaptation capacity as the global north.(#_ftn18) Equality among unequal actors entrenches injustices, and in this case, silently affirms the destruction of SIDS as they stand on the climate frontline.

Mirroring the rhetoric of Black feminist literature, does it not make sense to center those disproportionately impacted and most at risk?(#_ftn19) If indigenous and racialized communities as a whole were safe from climate change, it would mean everyone else would have to be safe, since their safety would necessitate the destruction of all systems of oppression.

With COP 31 approaching, we have the opportunity to radically and unapologetically center their historical and contemporary experiences of climate change and related injustices. The time is right for a new era of environmentally just climate action, and the opportunities are endless. Let us start by moving beyond limited metrics towards new climate goals, built on proportional responsibility, the deconstruction of systems of violence, and the lessons taught to us by those who came before.

Dyin’ fair is not symbolic. In a finite world, shaped by unequal histories and driven by extractive structures, it is the bedrock of any movement.
