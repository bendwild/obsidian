Decolonization is a powerful word. It can simultaneously elicit feelings of shame, anger, and fear; inspire collective action, solidarity, and purpose; signal historical injustice, and demand accountability… It speaks the tongue of a deeply social, political, and moral language and holds the power to legitimize or destabilize movements and institutions. Its deep historical roots and promises of a different future have given rise to an entire field of academics and advocates seeking to decolonize institutions, nations, systems, and processes. Decolonize science, the syllabus, the body, the internet, philosophy, history, medicine, even God… I have heard it all. The ease with which the language of decolonization has been adopted is both inspiring and dangerous. As Tuck and Yang note, “_the easy absorption, adoption, and transposing of decolonization is yet another form of settler appropriation_”.1 It misconstrues decolonization as a metaphor and, in doing so, hollows out its epistemological basis.

Therefore, it is essential to define decolonization and examine its historical origins to not desacralize its value. The first use of the word dates back to Moritz Joseph Browns’ 1932 article titled ‘_Imperialism’_, where he refers to the gradual loosening of imperial political control over colonies.2 Moritz, along with other scholars such as Gardinier, while not in agreement regarding the chronology of decolonization, understood it primarily as a global-scale political transformation, most intense and successful in the three decades following World War II.2 As this transformation accelerated and most former colonies gained formal independence, the term came to denote the historical period during which, and the process through which, colonial rule was abrogated and sovereignty was transferred.3

Yet political independence did not dissolve the structures colonizing powers had left behind. In the post-colonial era, formerly colonized nations continued to be confronted by the enduring legacies of empire. Under these circumstances, the meaning of decolonization necessarily evolved. No longer confined to the political sphere, decolonization began to incorporate the struggle to dismantle the legacies of a broader colonial experience, including its socio-cultural, economic, and psychological aspects.2 Decolonization, in its many forms, provided a solution to the paradox of the persistence of inequality, dependency, and marginalization in the absence of the colonizer.5

Various interpretations exist on what post-colonial decolonization entails. Decolonization inherently leaves room for differing chronologies, narratives, and consequences.5 In general, scholars understand decoloniality as a praxis of ‘undoing and redoing’.6 Whether this means restitution, abolition, economic liberation, or all of the above depends on the perspective of the “decolonizer”. Personally, I particularly appreciate Mahmood Mamdani’s interpretation, describing it as “_not simply independence from outside rule. It is an act of thinking, of imagination. It means dreaming up a political community that undoes the organic link between state and nation that has gelled over the past five centuries”._7

The utility of the term decolonization is hindered by its celebrity status in popular culture, which has rendered it rather devoid of meaning, and, more pressingly, by its inherent assumptions. Decolonization evokes the colonial, the powerlessness, cultural denigration, and its undoing. However, we are no longer colonized, no longer powerless. I don’t deny we are inhabiting a world rigged against us with extractive, unjust, and chronic power structures. However, something has changed. We have a choice. No one is holding a gun to our heads, cutting off our hands if we don’t collect enough rubber, or shipping us across the Atlantic. Invoking decolonization lightly risks trivializing and tokenizing a history so brutal and fraught with violence, and is almost an insult to the struggle for liberation of these nations.

Additionally, a value judgement is added to the concept of decolonization. If you decolonize, it must be automatically better. However, the [[imperial core]], with its powerful financial institutions, business moguls, and multinational corporations, may have benefitted at least as much from decolonization as it once did from colonization.8  Colonies were freed into a world with a so-called global rules-based order, with rules set and policed by their former colonizers (as seen with the US breaking global rules as it “defends the free world”). Operating in this globalized world, where capital is highly mobile, the deeply fragmented, politically insecure, and vulnerable global south has become an easy prey.2  

I recognize this is a controversial statement and have been struggling myself with how to give this idea form. It is certainly not a perfect argument; however, I think the value of this viewpoint lies in examining decolonization, who it serves, and what it aims to accomplish. I feel that, ironically, the main actors in the push for decolonization are the wealthy, powerful, white institutions that intend to decolonize themselves, out of goodwill or in a move to innocence. While these gestures signal progress, they often fail to challenge deeper structural inequalities and risk being limited to a symbolic branding exercise. Additionally, these acts of decolonization mirror the same power dynamics we want to dismantle. Attempts to decolonize are often implemented top-down by Western actors, inadvertently reinforcing the very binary they aim to dismantle and implying that decolonization must be authorized by those already in power.

Not to throw out the baby with the bathwater, I turn to global health aid architecture in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) to explore what decolonization could look like from an African perspective. The challenges to healthy lives in SSA are many, exacerbated by health expenditure that remains among the lowest in the world.9 Approximately $92 per person per year is spent on health in SSA, and 23 of 46 countries in the region rely on external financing for more than a third of their health expenditures.9 Meanwhile, global health financing is facing its biggest crisis to date, with major funders such as the US and many European nations introducing unprecedented funding cuts. And of course, those most affected are not the loudest voices; Western researchers and institutions, but it is the patients of under-resourced health systems in SSA.

This has left the field in a no-man’s land. The immense progress achieved over the past decades risks being completely undone in just a few years. However, I believe it also provides an opportunity. A chance to imagine a radical future, rethink what global health means, and whether we even want to continue working under this denominator. Without being as dependent on funding, and the methods, ideologies, and priorities that come with it, there has emerged breathing space to decolonize from an African perspective.

Of course, in practice, this can mean a plentitude of things. However, I believe many would agree on these core aspects:

1.     **Resist imposed universality**. African problems require African solutions.  The assumption that one model fits all, [[the tyranny of the global]], must be dismantled.10

2.     **True accountability**. African leaders, NGOs, and supra-national organizations must face repercussions for false promises and corruption. Otherwise, decolonization risks becoming an empty catchphrase.

3.     **Overcome colonial trauma**. [[Learned helplessness]] and dependence can be undone. Scapegoating colonial times and the West cannot become an excuse for perpetuating systems of dependence and oppression.  

4.     **Reclaim [[knowledge production]]**. Africa does not lack the knowledge or expertise; it lacks ownership, authorship, and institutional power.  

5.     **Structural refinancing**. Financing mechanisms must shift from donor-controlled models to African-led financing pools.

And most importantly, this doesn’t call for the abolition of Western (or Eastern) relationships, but rather for a renewed way of partnership. One where governance institutionalizes African agency. _If decolonization is not led by Africa or other former colonies, who is it really for?_
