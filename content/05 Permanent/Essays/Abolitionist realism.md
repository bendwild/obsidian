Ugo Felicia Edu traces her intellectual and personal journey in understanding the dynamics of race and aesthetics embedded in health in M_editations on race, health, and aesthetics_.1 Edu reflects on her time as a researcher, but more blatantly, as a Black woman in Brazil, and uses these lived experiences as a vantage point to narrate historical and current (mis)constructions of health.  The mapping of everything bad, unhealthy, or unwanted on Black (female) bodies has deep roots in Brazilian society and history. Historically, bodies have been measured, ranked, and moralized according to race and background using scientific racism and biotypology as epistemological legitimacy. These value judgements reverberate in contemporary crises such as the mortality crisis among Black women in the United States, who were, until very recently, routinely blamed for their own poor health outcomes. Through epistemicide, Black and Indigenous knowledge was, and still is, delegitimized and displaced by colonial and Eurocentric knowledge structures, further denying a seat at the table for Black bodies. She discusses her experiences as a Black woman receiving care from white physicians, talking about her research to these and other white actors, and illustrates how so-called best practices remain subject to an aesthetic value system, and how participation in [[knowledge production]] is legitimized or undermined by color. It begs the question: “Whose health depends on racism?”

_[[Abolitionist Reimaginings of Health]]_ builds upon this narrative anthropological review on aesthetics and health with a call to action.2 With the central premise that racial structural violence is a public health crisis, the authors argue that incremental reforms within existing medical systems are insufficient. They push for radical creativity in imagining solutions and for finding new ways to address harm and deconstruct oppressive systems. Through the lens of [[narrative medicine]], an abolitionist reimagining of health includes structural competency as an approach to recognize upstream sources (policing, incarceration, [[organized abandonment]]) of downstream adverse medical outcomes, as well as the prevention of violence and the dismantling of carceral/punitive practices. Abolitionist medicine provides the tools to respond to the structural and historical violence reproduced by the (US) health care system and provide care without oppression.

I would like to take a closer look at abolitionist medicine, as it is a new concept for me and has made me reflect on what it truly means to be healthy and/or to provide equitable health care. Traditional public health education has taught me that upstream solutions are the key to overcoming health inequity. However, these readings tell us that our classic public health river analogy has always been a lie, or at the very least, an incomplete truth. The analogy works when the problems are technical. Housing, nutrition, exercise… It fails when the problem becomes more intangible. Racism, poverty, and trauma are not confined to these simple categories. Born out of compound injustices at every level of the system, these elements have impacts upstream, midstream, and downstream, and don’t heal with single interventions. They are inherent to the structures we live in.

Recognizing the need to think further than the confines of the system, and thus the need for abolition medicine, the friction for me lies in what it means to practice this new philosophy. If healthiness is a value produced and mapped through an aesthetic system, as Ugo argues, how can we destroy this global system that continues to affect our collective imaginations? I have always understood abolition as the act of dismantling a structure and find myself conflicted, perhaps with a lack of imagination, how a new system birthed from the ruins of its predecessor, yet governed by many of the same forces as the latter would prove to be different. Quoting Ruth Wilson Gilmore, the authors remind us:  _“Abolition is not absence; it is presence…. So those who feel in their gut deep anxiety that abolition means knock it all down, scorch the earth, and start something new, let that go.”_

This has given abolition a new meaning for me. Perhaps abolition, less than a singular act of destruction, is about creating new structures, from margin to center, that make the original institutions obsolete. Even beyond this, it is about the intermediary process before actualization, the sustained practice of speculation before “the revolution”. It reminds me of the book _Capitalist Realism_, in which Mark Fisher argues that it has now become easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of capitalism. How can an economic revolution be actualized if we can not even imagine a world post-capitalism? Similarly, through dreaming of a radical future and re-narrating a more racially just medicine, maybe we can begin to untether medicine from its violent inheritances.

To finish on a tangent, even though Edu does not explicitly mention abolition, I feel like her meditations mirror its philosophy. The way she wrote this piece resists institutional/academic expectations, centers the narrative, and, through the messiness of it all, exposes a discomfort necessary for reimagining.  Her opening statement perfectly lays this out for me: _“I can’t promise that it will be straightforward. Why do you have that expectation? But if you’ll allow me, I’ll take you along.”_ As health providers/public health professionals, we must be willing to be taken along in this story, ask ourselves what our oath to do no harm truly means, and, through reflection and imagination, act upon this future.
